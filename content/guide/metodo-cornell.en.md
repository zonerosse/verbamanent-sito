The Cornell method is a way of taking notes that turns the page into a study tool. It isn't just for writing during the lecture: it is built from the ground up for reviewing and testing yourself.

It was devised by **Walter Pauk**, a professor at Cornell University, who described it in his book *How to Study in College*. It is still one of the most widely used methods at universities around the world.

## How to divide the page

The page is split into three areas, always the same way.

| Area | Where | Rough size (Letter or A4 sheet) | What goes in it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Notes** | Wide column on the right | About two thirds of the width | What the lecturer explains: concepts, definitions, examples |
| **Cues: keywords and questions** | Narrow column on the left | About one third (around 2.5 inches) | Keywords and the questions your notes answer |
| **Summary** | Strip along the bottom | About 2 inches (5 cm) high | The gist of the page in two or three sentences |

At the top, above the three areas, always write **subject, topic and date**: in two months you'll need it to find the right lecture.

## The 5 Rs: how to use it

Pauk summed up the method in five steps, known as the "5 Rs", each with its own moment.

| R | What you do | When |
|---|---|---|
| **Record** | Write the main ideas in the notes column, in your own words | During the lecture |
| **Reduce** | In the left column, write keywords and questions | Right after the lecture |
| **Recite** | Cover the notes, read the questions and answer out loud | Right after, then at every review |
| **Reflect** | Connect the topic to what you already know and write the summary at the bottom | The same day |
| **Review** | Every so often, go through the questions again, even for just a few minutes | In the following days and weeks |

The heart of the method is the third step: quizzing yourself with the left column. That is what makes it so effective, because trying to remember strengthens memory far more than rereading.

## A complete example

Here is what a Cornell page might look like after a biology lecture.

<div class="cornell-es">
  <div class="tit">Plant biology · Photosynthesis <span>16 October</span></div>
  <div class="cue">
    <span class="etich">Cues: keywords and questions</span>
    <p>What is photosynthesis?</p>
    <p>Where does it happen?</p>
    <p>What goes in and what comes out?</p>
    <p>What are the two stages?</p>
    <p>Why does it matter to us?</p>
  </div>
  <div class="note">
    <span class="etich">Notes</span>
    <p>Process by which plants turn light energy into chemical energy (sugars).</p>
    <p>In the <b>chloroplasts</b>, mostly in leaves. Pigment: <b>chlorophyll</b> (green).</p>
    <p>In: CO₂ + water + light → out: glucose + O₂.<br>6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂</p>
    <p>1) <b>Light reactions</b>: use light, make ATP and release O₂.<br>2) <b>Calvin cycle</b> (light-independent): uses ATP to fix CO₂ into sugars.</p>
    <p>Produces the oxygen we breathe and is the base of almost every food chain.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="sum">
    <span class="etich">Summary</span>
    In their chloroplasts, plants use light to turn CO₂ and water into sugars and oxygen, in two stages: the light reactions and the Calvin cycle. Without photosynthesis there would be no oxygen and no food.
  </div>
</div>
<p class="didascalia">To review: cover the right column and try to answer the questions on the left.</p>

A few things to notice:

- the notes use **short phrases**, symbols and arrows, not full sentences;
- the questions on the left are **real questions** you can answer out loud;
- the summary **doesn't repeat the notes**: it condenses them in a few lines, in new words.

## On paper, laptop or tablet

The method works on any medium.

- **On paper**: draw a vertical line about a third of the way across and a horizontal one near the bottom. You can also buy notebooks pre-printed with the Cornell layout.
- **On a laptop**: in Word or Google Docs, a two-column table with a merged last row for the summary is all you need.
- **On a tablet**: handwriting apps let you use a Cornell template as the background of every page.

## Pros and cons

| Pros | Cons |
|---|---|
| Every page is ready for self-testing | Takes a few minutes of work after the lecture |
| Forces you to rework ideas instead of copying | In very fast lectures the notes column fills up quickly |
| The summaries at the bottom become a digest of the whole course | Subjects with lots of formulas or graphs need more space |

## Which subjects it suits best

Cornell shines in theory-heavy subjects full of concepts, definitions and connections: law, history, biology, economics, medicine, philosophy. In very technical subjects with lots of exercises, use it for the theory and keep the exercises on separate sheets.

For review, the questions in the left column turn easily into [flashcards](/en/guides/how-to-study-with-flashcards/). And if you want a complete method for before, during and after the lecture, read the guide on [how to take notes in college](/en/guides/how-to-take-notes-in-college/).
