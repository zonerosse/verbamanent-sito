For students with dyslexia and other specific learning difficulties, the concept map is one of the most used assistive tools: the Italian 2011 guidelines list it next to tables and formula sheets. But a badly made map is just a drawn summary, and in a test it doesn't help, or the teacher won't accept it.

Here you'll find what a concept map is (and how it differs from a mind map), how to make one step by step, the rules for bringing it to a test and the tools to make it. For the bigger picture on tools and rights, see the guide to [assistive tools and exemptions](/en/guides/assistive-tools-dyslexia/).

## Concept map or mind map?

They're often used as synonyms, but they are two different things.

| | Concept map | Mind map |
|---|---|---|
| **Who invented it** | Joseph Novak, Cornell University, 1970s | Tony Buzan, 1970s |
| **Shape** | Top-down: general concepts above, details below | Radial: the topic in the middle, branches around it |
| **Links** | Arrows with a **linking word** ("causes", "is made of", "splits into") | Branches with no words on the lines |
| **Good for** | Understanding the **relationships** between concepts | Collecting ideas and recalling at a glance |

For studying and for tests the concept map works better, because the linking words force you to understand *how* the concepts fit together. The mind map is great for a first review of a lecture.

## Do they really work?

Yes, and the research also says when. A large meta-analysis by Nesbit and Adesope (2006), covering 55 studies and almost 6,000 students, found that using concept maps goes with better retention. A more recent meta-analysis (Schroeder and colleagues, 2018) confirmed the result and added an important detail: on average **building the map yourself works better than studying a ready-made one**.

The reason is the same as for self-testing: to decide what to link and with which word, your brain has to process the material. A copied map doesn't do that work.

## How to make a concept map, step by step

1. **Start from a question**: "What is a contract?", "Why did the French Revolution break out?". Novak calls it the *focus question*: it keeps the map on point.
2. **List the key concepts**: 10-15 words, not sentences. Take them from slide titles, words in bold, what the lecturer stressed.
3. **Put them in order**: the most general concept at the top, the more specific ones below.
4. **Link them with arrows and write the linking word**: every link should form a sentence you can read, like "Contract → *requires* → Agreement".
5. **Add an example** to the hardest concepts: a case, a formula, a date.
6. **Read the sentences back**: if a link doesn't read as a true sentence, fix it.
7. **Review it out loud**: cover part of it and try to explain the links.

## Rules for students with dyslexia

A few tricks make the map easier to read and remember:

- **key words, not sentences**: three or four words per box at most;
- **a large, clear font**, always the same, with plenty of space between boxes;
- **few colours, each with a fixed meaning**: for example blue for definitions, green for examples, orange for dates;
- **images and symbols** where they help memory, without filling the page;
- **one map per topic**: three simple maps are better than one giant one;
- **always A4**, if you'll bring it to a test.

## The map in a test

In Italy the map is an assistive tool set out in the personalised plan, but in a test it only works if it's clear what it may contain. Many teachers ask for:

| What teachers often ask for | Why |
|---|---|
| **Key words only**, no full sentences | Otherwise the map becomes the answer |
| **Formulas and values** in a separate box | They're what memory lets down most |
| **Seeing it before** the test, sometimes signing it | To check it's a map and not a summary |
| **Name, class and date** at the top | To recognise it and keep it with the test |

The most useful tip: **agree on the rules with your teacher at the start of the year**, not on test day. And bring a sample map: it's the fastest way to find out what they accept.

## What to make it with

| Tool | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| **Pen and paper** | Drawing it by hand helps memory | Slow to correct and redo neatly |
| **Mapping software** (for example CmapTools, free, from Novak's group) | Everything can be moved and corrected, prints clean | You have to learn to use it |
| **Apps that build it from the lecture** | The base is ready in seconds | It needs reviewing: a map really works when you work on it yourself |

Whatever you use, remember what the research says: the map helps most **while you build it**. If you start from a ready-made map, make it yours: remove, add, change the linking words.

## The most common mistakes

- Writing full sentences in the boxes.
- Linking with arrows but no words: it's unclear *why* two concepts are connected.
- Putting everything in a single map.
- Using ten different colours with no meaning.
- Bringing a map to a test that the teacher has never seen.
- Studying from someone else's map without reworking it.
