Sooner or later most students wish they had the lecture **in writing**: every word the lecturer said, to check a passage, catch up on a missed class or study in peace. In some countries it's a real tradition: in Italy, for instance, students on long courses such as Medicine and Law split the lectures between them and transcribe them word for word.

This guide covers how to transcribe a lecture, how to do it in very little time with artificial intelligence, how to organise transcripts as a group and what to watch out for.

## What a good lecture transcript is

A lecture transcript turns **the audio of a lecture into written text**. A good one isn't just raw transcription, though. It's usually tidied up: the lecturer's repetitions and filler words go, headings are added, slides or whiteboard drawings are put in.

## Transcribing by hand: the traditional way

The traditional method is simple but slow:

1. **record the lecture** with your phone or a recorder, sitting close to the lecturer;
2. **listen back in small chunks**, pausing after every sentence to type;
3. **reread and tidy up** the text, adding headings and slides.

The problem is time: transcribing an hour of lecture by hand takes several hours of work. That's why groups of students share the job.

## Transcribing with artificial intelligence

Today the longest part, writing down what you hear, can be done by AI in a few minutes. The job becomes:

1. **record well**: audio quality decides transcript quality. In the classroom, sit near the lecturer; for online lectures, record the computer's audio, not a microphone picking up the speakers;
2. **have the audio transcribed** by speech recognition software, set to the lecturer's language;
3. **check the critical points**: proper names, technical terms, numbers, formulas. These are the most frequent errors;
4. **tidy up and structure**: headings, lists, slides in the right place.

## Sharing transcripts as a group

When a class splits up the transcribing, a few rules prevent chaos.

| What to agree on | Example |
|---|---|
| **Rota** | A shared calendar: who transcribes which lecture |
| **One template** | The same format for everyone: title, date, lecturer, topics |
| **Deadline** | Every transcript ready within 48 hours of the lecture |
| **Review** | A second classmate rereads it before it's shared |
| **Shared folder** | One folder only, with files named by date |

A tip: whoever transcribes a lecture learns it best, because they have to listen to it again carefully. When it's your turn, make the most of it.

## From transcript to notes

A transcript is very handy for catching up on a missed lecture or checking a passage. But **studying only from transcripts** has a limit: two hours of lecture become dozens of pages, and rereading them is slow and not very effective.

The best approach is to use them to build your own notes: key ideas, questions, summary. The method is in the guide on [how to take notes in college](/en/guides/how-to-take-notes-in-college/), and for review [flashcards](/en/guides/how-to-study-with-flashcards/) work brilliantly.

## Can you record a lecture? What the rules say

Two things to know before recording and transcribing.

- **Permission.** Many universities regulate the recording of lectures in their policies, and many lecturers ask to be told. The right thing is always to ask.
- **The lecturer's rights.** In many countries a lecture is protected by copyright like any other work: Italian copyright law, for example, explicitly covers oral teaching works. Recording and transcribing for your own study is one thing; **selling transcripts** or posting them online without the lecturer's consent is another, and may infringe their rights.
