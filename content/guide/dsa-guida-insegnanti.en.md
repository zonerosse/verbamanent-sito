According to Ministry data, students certified with a specific learning disorder (in Italy: **DSA**) are about **6%** of the total (school year 2022/23; they were 0.9% in 2010/11): in a class of 25, one or two on average. They are not asking for a discount: they are asking to be assessed on what they know, not on how fast they read or how many spelling mistakes they make. This guide is for teachers: what the rules say, how to write a personalised plan (PDP) that really works, how to prepare accessible lessons and tests, what to do about maps, extra time and assessment. At the end, the questions teachers ask most often.

## The rules in five lines

- **Law 170 of 2010** recognises dyslexia, dysorthographia, dysgraphia and dyscalculia and gives students the right to **assistive tools**, **exemptions** and **appropriate forms of testing and assessment**, from school to university (art. 5).
- The **guidelines of 12 July 2011** explain how to apply it and introduce the **PDP**, the *Piano Didattico Personalizzato*, written by the class council by the end of the first term.
- **Legislative Decree 62 of 2017** states that the assessment of students with SpLDs is **consistent with the PDP**, including at state exams (arts. 11 and 20).
- The school also has a duty of **identification**: if difficulties persist despite targeted support, it must inform the family (art. 3). It does not diagnose, but it is often the first to notice.
- The measures apply **in every subject** where they are needed, not only in Italian and maths.

## Assistive tools and exemptions: the difference

| | What they do | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Assistive tools** (*strumenti compensativi*) | Replace or ease the task made difficult by the disorder | Text-to-speech, lesson recording, maps and formula sheets, calculator, computer with spell checker, dictation |
| **Exemptions** (*misure dispensative*) | Release the student from tasks that are very hard because of the disorder and do not improve learning | No reading aloud in class, no copying from the board, extra time, fewer exercises, no rote learning of lists |

The full list with examples is in the guide [Assistive tools and exemptions](/en/guides/assistive-tools-dyslexia/).

## Writing a PDP that really works

The PDP is not a form to fill in once: it is the agreement on how that student will learn and be assessed. Some practical criteria:

1. **Start from the diagnosis**, in particular the recommendations for the school, and from a talk with the family and, from secondary school on, with the student.
2. **Write the measures subject by subject.** "Use of the calculator" is not enough: in maths yes, in physics yes, in the times-tables test no? Write it down.
3. **State the moments**: lessons, homework, written tests, oral tests.
4. **Be concrete about tests**: how much extra time or how many fewer exercises, who reads the instructions, how maps are used, when tests are scheduled.
5. **Write how work is assessed**: content rather than form, and what happens in foreign languages.
6. **Plan a review** mid-year: the law says measures must be monitored, and changed if they do not work.
7. **Use the student's own tools**: if they already use text-to-speech or maps at home, start there. A new tool introduced on the day of the test slows them down instead of helping.

Many families arrive at the meeting with a proposal already written: our [PDP template](/en/guides/pdp-template/) is designed for exactly that. It is only a basis for discussion: the decision remains with the class council.

## In class: small choices that help everyone

Many things that help a student with an SpLD make the lesson clearer for the whole class:

- **Instructions written and spoken**, one at a time, in short sentences.
- **Digital materials with real text**: a PDF made from a scan is an image, and text-to-speech cannot read it. Export documents from Word or Google Docs, or share the original file.
- **Slides and materials in advance**, so the student can listen to them beforehand or follow them with text-to-speech.
- **A legible font**: sans serif, at least 12-14 points, generous line spacing, no text all in capitals or italics, left-aligned.
- **A map or outline at the start** of the lesson, and a summary at the end.
- **No surprise reading aloud** in front of the class: for a student with dyslexia it is a public test, not an exercise.
- **No fast dictation and no copying from the board**: photocopies, files or photos of the board instead.
- **Allow the lesson to be recorded**, if the PDP provides for it: recording is an assistive tool mentioned in the guidelines.

## In tests: what matters

| Measure | How to put it into practice |
|---|---|
| **Extra time** | Often 30% more. Alternatively, **fewer exercises** with the same objectives: in many classes this is easier to manage |
| **Instructions read aloud** | Read by the teacher, or listened to with text-to-speech (headphones) |
| **Scheduled tests** | Dates known in advance, avoiding several tests on the same day |
| **Maps and formula sheets** | Prepared by the student beforehand, seen by the teacher, available during the test |
| **Oral instead of written** | When writing does not let the student show what they know, especially in foreign languages |
| **Accessible test sheet** | Large font, space, one question per line, no double instructions in the same sentence |
| **Structured tests** | Multiple choice, true/false, gap fill: less writing for the same content |
| **Technology** | Computer with spell checker, calculator, text-to-speech, if provided for in the PDP |

A tip: **prepare one test for everyone**, then adapt it (font, fewer exercises, instructions read aloud). It is less work than a different test and the student does not feel "set apart".

## "But isn't the map cheating?"

It is the most frequent question, and an understandable one. The answer is no: the concept map is an **assistive tool** provided for by the guidelines. It compensates for the difficulty of **retrieving from memory** information such as words, dates, formulas and sequences; it does not replace studying. To be used in a test, a map should:

- be **prepared by the student**, not downloaded or made by someone else;
- contain **key words, formulas and diagrams**, not whole sentences or copied paragraphs;
- be **seen by the teacher before the test** (many sign or initial it);
- fit on **one sheet**, usually A4.

If the map already contains the answers to the questions, the problem is usually the test, not the map: questions that ask students to **reason, connect and explain** can be answered with key words, but only if the topic has been understood. The guide [Concept maps for dyslexia](/en/guides/concept-maps-dyslexia/) explains how to make a good test map.

## Assessing content, not form

For students with SpLDs, assessment must be **consistent with the PDP**. In practice:

- **spelling mistakes do not lower the mark** in tests that assess content. If a test assesses spelling itself, say so beforehand and take the disorder into account;
- **handwriting is not assessed**: if it is illegible, the student can use block capitals or the computer, or read back what they wrote;
- **in foreign languages**, oral work is preferred. The PDP can provide for **exemption from written tests**: at the state exam the written paper is replaced by an oral test and the diploma remains valid. In severe cases, at the family's request, **exemption from the subject** is possible, with a differentiated programme and, in upper secondary school, a certificate instead of the diploma;
- **in oral tests**, give time to answer, accept short answers if correct, and do not penalise difficulty remembering names and dates if the concept is clear.

### State exam and INVALSI

At the exam the student uses **the same assistive tools as in the PDP** and has **more time** for the written papers: that is why it matters that the PDP describes well what was used during the year. In the INVALSI tests, according to the PDP, students may have **up to 15 extra minutes per test**, the **"voice donor"** (audio reading), the **calculator** and the **dictionary**.

## The "The tools I use" sheet

More and more students come to their teacher with a sheet listing the assistive tools they use: text-to-speech, lesson recording, a test map, extra time in tests. It is a good starting point for a conversation, especially with someone who was not there when the PDP was written (a substitute, a new teacher).

## Frequently asked questions

**How much extra time should I give?**
The PDP must say. 30% extra is often used, which is the figure given by the guidelines for university exams. Alternatively, fewer exercises in the same time.

**Isn't it unfair on the other students?**
No: the tools do not give an advantage, they remove a disadvantage. A classmate without an SpLD does not go faster with text-to-speech. Explaining this to the class, if the student agrees, helps avoid comments.

**Can the student record the lesson?**
Yes, if the PDP provides for it: recording is an assistive tool. It is for personal study only: it must not be shared or published.

**What if a student has difficulties but no diagnosis?**
The school must offer targeted support and, if difficulties persist, inform the family so they can get an assessment. In the meantime the class council can still adopt personalised measures for students with special educational needs, under the 2012 ministerial guidance: it is a different route from Law 170.

**The student does not want to use the tools in front of classmates.**
This is common, especially in secondary school. It helps to make the tools normal: digital materials for everyone, headphones allowed for everyone in listening activities, maps offered to the whole class. And to ask the student, privately, what would make them more comfortable.

**Can the PDP be changed during the year?**
Yes, and it should be if something does not work. The law requires the measures to be monitored periodically.

## The most common mistakes

- **A PDP copied** from a template, the same for every student with an SpLD.
- **Measures written but not applied** in tests, or applied only by some teachers.
- **Marking spelling** in a history essay.
- **Refusing the map** as "cheating" without having seen it beforehand.
- **Surprise tests** and unscheduled oral tests.
- **Scanned PDF materials**, which text-to-speech cannot read.
