You have your child's diagnosis of a specific learning disorder (in Italy: **DSA**, *disturbo specifico dell'apprendimento*), or the school has told you they struggle more than others with reading or writing. What now? This guide puts the steps in order: who makes the diagnosis, how to hand it in at school, what the **PDP** is (the *Piano Didattico Personalizzato*, the personalised learning plan), who writes it, what to check before signing, how tests, INVALSI and the state exam work, and how to help at home without becoming a back-up teacher.

The rules come from Italian **Law 170 of 2010**, the **guidelines of 12 July 2011** and **Legislative Decree 62 of 2017** on assessment. Sources are at the end.

## In short: the steps in order

| # | What to do | When |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Get the assessment and the **diagnosis** | As soon as difficulties persist despite help at school |
| 2 | **Hand in the diagnosis** at the school office, with a registration (protocol) number | Straight away. For the last year of lower or upper secondary school, by **31 March** |
| 3 | Meet the **class council** or the class coordinator | Before the PDP is written |
| 4 | Read and **sign the PDP** | By the end of the **first term** |
| 5 | Check that the PDP **is applied** | All year round, at every test |
| 6 | **Review the PDP** | Every year, and whenever something does not work |

## First of all: what SpLDs are

There are four **specific learning disorders**: **dyslexia** (reading), **dysorthographia** (spelling), **dysgraphia** (handwriting) and **dyscalculia** (numbers and calculation). People often have more than one.

Two things to know, and to tell your child:

- **they have nothing to do with intelligence**. The law says so clearly: SpLDs occur "in the presence of adequate cognitive abilities";
- **they do not go away**, but with the right tools they weigh much less. The aim is not a "cure", it is studying on equal terms.

## 1. The diagnosis: who makes it and what it contains

The diagnosis is made by the **national health service**: usually child neuropsychiatry or the developmental age service of your local health authority (ASL). In regions where the public service cannot do it in time, the law allows it to be made by **specialists or centres accredited** by the region. Each region has its own rules: before booking a private assessment, ask the ASL or the school which centres are recognised, otherwise you risk a diagnosis the school cannot accept.

The school **does not diagnose**. It can, however, notice the difficulties: if they persist after targeted support, the law asks the school to inform the family. If they tell you, it is not an accusation: it is the first step towards getting the tools.

What the diagnostic report contains:

- the **type of disorder**, with its international classification code;
- the **functioning profile**: how your child reads, writes and calculates, their strengths and weaknesses;
- **recommendations for the school**: the assistive tools and exemptions advised.

That last part is precious: it is the basis of the PDP. If the report does not include it, ask the specialist to add it.

### Does the diagnosis expire?

The disorder does not expire, but the **functioning profile** must be updated: as a rule **when moving from one school cycle to the next** (for example from primary to secondary) and in any case not earlier than three years after the previous one. When your child changes school, the new school must activate the measures straight away with the documents it has, without waiting for the update.

## 2. Handing in the diagnosis at school

1. **Take the diagnosis to the school office** and ask for it to be registered. Keep a copy with the registration number: it proves the date you handed it in.
2. **Ask who the SpLD contact person is** at the school (many schools call this role the inclusion coordinator) and who coordinates your child's class.
3. **Ask when the class council meets** for the PDP and whether you can attend or send your observations beforehand.

**Watch the deadlines in exam years.** For students in the **last year of lower secondary** (3ª media) or **upper secondary** (5ª superiore), the diagnosis must be handed in **by 31 March**: after that date the school can no longer accept it for that year's exam.

**When changing school**, the diagnosis and the PDP do not move to the new school automatically: your written consent is needed, or you hand them in again yourself. It is best to do it at enrolment, not in September.

## 3. The PDP: what it is, who writes it, by when

The **personalised learning plan** is the document in which the school writes down, in black and white, how it will teach and assess your child. It is a commitment by the school, not a favour.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Who writes it** | The **class council** (in primary school, the team of teachers), together with the family. The specialists who follow your child can also contribute |
| **Who signs it** | The **head teacher**, the teachers and the **family** |
| **By when** | By the end of the **first term** of the school year (usually by December). For a diagnosis arriving during the year, as soon as possible |
| **How long it lasts** | One year. It is reviewed every year and can be **changed during the year** if something does not work |
| **Who keeps it** | The school, in the confidential file. You are entitled to a **copy** |

According to the guidelines, the PDP must contain at least:

| What it contains | In practice |
|---|---|
| Student details and description of the disorder | From the diagnosis |
| Individualised and personalised teaching activities | How the class works with them |
| **Assistive tools** | Text-to-speech, maps, calculator, computer… and in which subjects |
| **Exemptions** | No reading aloud, no copying from the board… and in which subjects |
| **Personalised forms of testing and assessment** | Extra time, scheduled oral tests, oral instead of written, assessing content rather than form |

The key words are **"in which subjects"**: a PDP that says "text-to-speech" without saying where and when it is used stays on paper. The measures apply to **every subject** where they are needed, not just Italian.

## 4. How to prepare for the meeting with the school

The PDP turns out much better if you arrive with clear ideas. Bring:

- the **diagnosis**, with the recommendations for the school highlighted;
- the list of **tools your child already uses**, and of those you would like to try;
- two or three **concrete examples**: homework that went wrong because of reading the instructions, a test left half-finished for lack of time;
- the **questions** you want to ask: how will tests be done? Who reads the instructions? Can the map be used? How is written English assessed?

If your child is old enough (from secondary school on), **bring them along** or ask what they want written. They are the one who will use those tools: a PDP they do not feel is theirs often goes unused.

## 5. Before signing the PDP, check

<div class="spunte">

- It lists the **tools your child really uses**, not a list copied from a template
- For each tool it says **in which subjects** and **at which moments** (lessons, homework, tests)
- It says **how tests are done**: extra time or fewer exercises, instructions read aloud, scheduled tests
- It says **how work is assessed**: content, not spelling or handwriting
- For **foreign languages** it is clear what happens with written work (preferring oral, exemption from written tests, possible exemption from the subject)
- If the **map in tests** is needed, it says how it is prepared and when the teacher sees it
- It names **who to contact** if something does not work (class coordinator, SpLD contact person)
- It sets a **date to review it** during the year
- You have a **copy**

</div>

If something does not convince you, **you do not have to sign straight away**: ask for it to be changed. Signing means you agree, so it is right to read it calmly.

## 6. If the PDP is not applied

It happens: a substitute teacher who does not know it, a teacher convinced the map is "cheating", a surprise test. What to do, in order:

1. **Talk to the teacher**, calmly and with the PDP in hand. That is often enough.
2. If not, talk to the **class coordinator** and the **SpLD contact person**.
3. If it is still not solved, write to the **head teacher** (by email or certified email, so there is a record), citing the signed PDP and what was not applied.
4. As a last resort, you can contact the **Regional School Office** or family associations (such as AID, the Italian Dyslexia Association), which offer advice desks.

Keep track of tests and marks: if a test was taken without the measures in the PDP, you can ask for it to be repeated with the measures provided.

## 7. Tests, INVALSI and the state exam

### Everyday tests

The most common measures, to be written in the PDP:

- **extra time** (often 30% more) or **fewer exercises** with the same objectives;
- **scheduled written and oral tests**, avoiding several tests on the same day;
- **instructions read aloud** by the teacher or by text-to-speech;
- **oral tests** instead of written ones when writing does not let the student show what they know;
- **maps and formula sheets** that can be consulted, prepared beforehand and seen by the teacher;
- **assessing content, not form**: spelling mistakes must not lower the mark.

### INVALSI tests

Students with SpLDs take the national INVALSI tests with the measures in their PDP: for example **up to 15 extra minutes per test**, the **"voice donor"** (the text read aloud through headphones), the **calculator** or the **dictionary**, if already used during the year. If the PDP provides for exemption from written foreign-language tests, the student may skip the English reading or listening section, or the whole English test.

### The state exam (end of lower and upper secondary school)

At the exam your child can use **the assistive tools in the PDP** and have **more time** for the written papers. The exam board takes the PDP into account. For foreign languages there are two very different routes:

| | What happens | The diploma |
|---|---|---|
| **Exemption from the written test** | The written foreign-language paper is replaced by an **oral test** | **Valid**, and it does not mention the exemption |
| **Exemption from the subject** (only in severe cases, at the family's request) | **Differentiated** programme and tests | In upper secondary school the student receives a **certificate** of credits, not the diploma |

Exemption from the subject has important consequences: choose it only if really necessary, after discussing it with the school and the specialist.

## 8. At home: helping without becoming the teacher

The most common risk is spending afternoons reading the books aloud for them. It works, but it tires everyone and does not make them independent. Better to give them tools they can use on their own:

- **text-to-speech** for long texts: the textbook in PDF, handouts, notes. Listening is not "cheating", it is the way they read;
- **audiobooks and digital books**: many publishers provide the digital version of textbooks, and there are digital libraries dedicated to people with SpLDs;
- **maps**: before studying a chapter, a map of the key words; afterwards, a map they make themselves to revise;
- **dictation**: for written homework, dictating and then listening back is much faster than writing by hand;
- **a routine**: homework at fixed times, short frequent breaks, difficult subjects when they are most rested.

Two pieces of advice that matter more than any tool: **praise effort, not just marks**, and **do not do the homework for them**. If homework takes hours every day, that is a signal to bring to the class council: the PDP can provide for reduced homework.

### Flexible hours at work

Law 170 provides that parents of students with SpLDs in the **first cycle** (primary and lower secondary school) who help them with homework are entitled to **flexible working hours**. How it applies is decided by national collective agreements: ask your HR office or trade union what yours provides.

## 9. Afterwards: towards university

Law 170 also applies at university. There is no PDP there: the student contacts the **disability and SpLD service** of their university, presenting the diagnosis. The guidelines provide for **up to 30% extra time** in exams, assistive tools and the option of preferring oral exams. Note: many universities ask for a **recent** diagnosis (often no older than three years if made when the student was a minor). It is worth updating it in the last year of upper secondary school. We cover this in detail in [Dyslexia at university](/en/guides/dyslexia-at-university/).

## The most common mistakes

- **Waiting.** Every year without tools is a year of effort and lost self-esteem. The sooner the diagnosis, the sooner the PDP.
- **Handing in the diagnosis verbally**, without registration: then there is no record of the date.
- **Signing a generic PDP**, the same for everyone, without subjects and without how tests are done.
- **Not involving your child**: a student who is ashamed of the tools does not use them.
- **Thinking the PDP is forever**: it must be reviewed every year, and changed when it does not work.
- **Choosing exemption from foreign languages without knowing its consequences** for the diploma.
