Sooner or later, anyone with dyslexia hears about **OpenDyslexic** and the other "dyslexia fonts", with heavier letters at the bottom. The promise is simple: change the font and read better. But is it true?

Here's what the research says, which typefaces to choose and, above all, the settings that matter more than the font: size, spacing, background. Easier reading is one of the assistive tools: the full picture is in the guide to [assistive tools and exemptions](/en/guides/assistive-tools-dyslexia/).

## Fonts designed for dyslexia

| Font | How it's designed | Licence |
|---|---|---|
| **OpenDyslexic** | Letters weighted at the bottom, to "anchor" them to the line and make them less alike | Free (SIL Open Font License) |
| **Dyslexie** | A similar idea: different shapes for letters that get mixed up (b/d, p/q) | Paid for many uses |
| **Atkinson Hyperlegible** | Made for people with low vision (Braille Institute): each letter is drawn not to resemble the others | Free |
| **Lexend** | Designed for faster reading, with wide letters and plenty of space | Free |

## What the research says

On "special" fonts the research is cooler than the promises:

- a study with Spanish readers with dyslexia, using eye-tracking (Rello and Baeza-Yates, 2013), **found no shorter reading times** with OpenDyslexic;
- a US study with students with dyslexia (Wery and Diliberto, 2017) **found no improvement** in reading speed or accuracy with OpenDyslexic;
- a Dutch study with children with and without dyslexia (Kuster and colleagues, 2018) found that the Dyslexie font **does not improve reading**, and the children preferred Arial.

At the same time, some students say they feel better with OpenDyslexic, mostly because of how the page looks. And comfort matters: if a font makes you want to read, that's already a plus.

**In short**: a special font is not a cure. If you like it, use it; if you don't, you're not missing anything.

## What really matters: spacing

A widely cited Italian and French study (Zorzi and colleagues, 2012), with 94 children with dyslexia, found a much clearer effect: with **more space between letters and words**, the children read about 20% faster and made half as many errors.

The explanation is **visual crowding**: in dyslexia, neighbouring letters interfere with each other more than usual. Widening the spacing reduces the interference.

## Recommended settings

Dyslexia style guides, such as the British Dyslexia Association's, agree on a few simple points:

| Setting | Advice |
|---|---|
| **Typeface** | Sans serif, simple: Arial, Verdana, Open Sans, or one of the fonts in the table above |
| **Size** | 12-14 pt or more; headings at least 20% larger |
| **Line spacing** | 1.5 |
| **Letter and word spacing** | A little wider than normal |
| **Background** | Plain colour, not glaring white: cream or a soft tint |
| **Italics, underline, ALL CAPS** | Avoid them for whole paragraphs: use **bold** for emphasis |
| **Alignment** | Left-aligned, not justified: uneven gaps between words are confusing |

## How to find your combination

Every reader is different. A practical way to choose:

1. **Take a real page** of what you're studying, not a sample text.
2. **Change one thing at a time**: size first, then spacing, then background, the font last.
3. **Read aloud for a minute** with each combination and note how many lines you read and how tired you got.
4. **Keep the best combination everywhere**: notes, digital books, flashcards. Switching every time is tiring.
5. **Add listening**: for long texts, text-to-speech removes much of the effort.

## The most common mistakes

- Thinking that changing the font is enough: spacing, size and background matter more.
- Using italics or ALL CAPS for emphasis.
- Justified text, with uneven gaps between words.
- A bright white background with small, thin letters.
- Changing settings every day instead of finding your combination and keeping it.
