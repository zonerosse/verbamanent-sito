Students with a specific learning difficulty (dyslexia, dysgraphia, dysorthography, dyscalculia) have the right to study with the right tools. In Italy this is set by **Law 170 of 2010**, and the **guidelines of 12 July 2011** explain how to apply it, at school and at university. The ideas behind them are useful everywhere.

Two terms keep coming up: **compensatory (assistive) tools** and **dispensatory measures (exemptions)**. They are often confused, but they are different things. Here is what they are, a list with examples, how the personalised plan works at school and what you can ask for at university.

## The difference in one line

| | What they do | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Assistive tools** | Replace or ease a task the difficulty makes hard | Text-to-speech reads the text for you |
| **Exemptions** | Excuse you from a task that is especially hard because of the difficulty and does not improve learning | Not reading aloud in class |

The guidelines put it this way: assistive tools are "teaching and technological tools that replace or ease the required task"; exemptions let the student "not carry out certain tasks" that, because of the disorder, "are particularly difficult and do not improve learning".

Neither is a shortcut: they level the playing field, so that what counts is what you know, not how fast you read or write.

## Assistive tools: the list

The guidelines give text-to-speech, the voice recorder, word processors with a spellchecker, the calculator, and lower-tech aids such as tables, formula sheets and concept maps as examples. In practice the list is longer:

| Tool | What it compensates for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Text-to-speech** | Reading: it turns a reading task into a listening task | Listening to the book or your notes instead of reading them |
| **Voice recorder** | Taking notes while listening | Recording the lecture and listening again at home |
| **Word processor with spellchecker** | Spelling and handwriting | Writing an essay on a computer |
| **Calculator** | Mental arithmetic | Using it in maths and physics problems |
| **Tables and formula sheets** | Remembering formulas, rules, times tables | The physics formula sheet during a test |
| **Concept maps** | Organising and recalling concepts | A map of the lecture to consult in an oral test |
| **Digital books and audiobooks** | Reading long texts | The textbook as a PDF, read by text-to-speech |
| **Voice dictation** | Writing | Dictating answers instead of writing them |
| **Highly legible fonts** | The visual effort of reading | Larger text, more line spacing, a soft background |

For students with dyslexia, the guidelines say, assistive tools are **above all those that turn reading into listening**.

## Exemptions: the list

Exemptions don't add a tool: they remove or adapt a task. The most common ones:

- **no reading aloud in class**, and no marks for activities where reading itself is what is assessed;
- **no copying from the board** and no fast dictation;
- **extra time** in tests: without more precise indications, the guidelines consider about **30% more** reasonable;
- **fewer exercises** with the same objectives, instead of more time;
- **scheduled oral tests** instead of surprise ones;
- **marking content, not form** in written work: spelling mistakes shouldn't lower the mark.

## At school: the personalised plan

At school in Italy, tools and exemptions are written down in the **PDP, the Personalised Learning Plan**. The class council prepares it together with the family, based on the diagnosis, by the end of the first term. According to the guidelines it must include at least:

| What the plan contains | |
|---|---|
| Student details and type of difficulty | From the diagnosis |
| Personalised teaching activities | How work is done in class |
| **Assistive tools** | Which ones, in which subjects |
| **Exemptions** | Which ones, in which subjects |
| Personalised testing and marking | How tests are set and marked |

A tip: bring the class council a **list of the tools you really use**, subject by subject. A good plan is much easier to write starting from what already works.

## At university

Law 170 also applies at university. The guidelines ask universities to:

- grant **extra time of up to 30%** in exams, or a reduction in the amount of written work;
- allow the tools already used at school: **lecture recording**, digital texts, **text-to-speech**, a computer with a spellchecker;
- **prefer oral exams** over written ones, taking the student's profile into account.

To ask for them, contact your university's **disability and SpLD service** with your diagnosis. Each university has its own procedure and deadlines: find out at the start of the course, not the week before the exam.

## How to choose the right tools

There's no list that suits everyone. A few practical criteria:

1. **Start from what costs you the most effort**: reading, writing, remembering formulas, organising concepts.
2. **Try them calmly, before tests**: a tool used for the first time on test day slows you down instead of helping.
3. **Agree on maps with your teacher**: many accept only key words, no sentences, and want to see them before the test.
4. **Keep the list up to date**: what you need in your first year of secondary school is not what you need at university.

## The most common mistakes

- Thinking that using the tools is "cheating": they are a right provided by law.
- Asking for them too late, right before exams.
- Test maps that are too full, with whole sentences: they become a summary and the teacher won't accept them.
- Using text-to-speech only to study and never to review: hearing a topic twice helps it stick.
- Not telling the teacher which tools you'll use in the test.
